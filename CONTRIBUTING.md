# How to contribute to a Humanitarians AI paper

Every paper in progress has its own public repository. Its README says what the paper is, where the draft is and what needs doing. Many also have a contributor guide (`CONTRIBUTING.md`) with paper-specific tasks. When a paper's own guide says something different from this page, follow the paper's guide. A model is [A New Shape for Work's guide](https://github.com/nikbearbrown/a-new-shape-for-work/blob/main/CONTRIBUTING.md).

## Getting started

1. **Read first.** Read the paper's README, then its draft, or its plan if there is no draft yet. You should know what the paper claims and what it doesn't.
2. **Choose one thing to do.** Pick one task from the paper's README or guide, and narrow it to one section, one source, one case or one result. Reviewing one section of the draft is always useful.
3. **Email Nik** at bear@bearbrown.co with the subject "*Paper title*: contributor". Include:
   - your name and your GitHub username;
   - the contribution you chose, and exactly what you will look at;
   - whether you hope to become an author, or want to help without authorship.

   Nik will add you to the paper's repository. GitHub then emails you an invitation, which you must accept within seven days.
4. **Claim the work.** Check the repository's open issues so you don't duplicate someone else's work. Then open an issue that says what you are taking on, and assign it to yourself.
5. **Make a branch** named `your-name/short-description`. Don't commit to `main`.
6. **Do the work,** one piece of work per branch.
7. **Open a pull request.** Say what you changed, how you checked it, and where you used an AI tool. Link your issue and request a review from `nikbearbrown`.
8. **Respond to the review** on the same branch until it is agreed and merged.
9. **Present your work** at Nik's Tuesday meeting or his office hours. Cover what you set out to do, what you did, what you found and the evidence for it, and what comes next. Do this each time you attend, even when the work is unfinished. Nik sends the meeting details when he adds you.
10. **Keep your record.** Your issues and pull requests are the record of what you did. The paper's contribution statement is drawn from them.

You can also help without access: anyone can open an issue on a public repository without emailing first.

## Recommend a journal Northeastern covers in full

Every contributor does this once for their paper. Northeastern's library has agreements that pay the open-access fee for papers whose corresponding author is at Northeastern, and Nik is the corresponding author.

1. Read [Open access publishing at Northeastern](https://library.northeastern.edu/resource_discovery/open-access-publishing/) and search [SciFree for Northeastern](https://search.scifree.se/northeastern).
2. Keep only journals whose fee is covered in full. A discount doesn't count, and some publishers cap the number of articles they cover each year.
3. Check each journal on the publisher's site: its scope, whether it accepts this kind of article, two or three recent papers close to this one, its policy on public drafts and preprints, and its practical limits.
4. Open an issue on the paper's repository titled "Venue recommendation: *journal name*", with one journal, a runner-up, your reasons, your links and the date you checked.

Confirm coverage yourself on the library's or the publisher's page. Don't rely on memory or on an AI tool's answer, because the agreements change.

## Credit

We follow [Yale's guidance on authorship](https://provost.yale.edu/policies/academic-integrity/guidance-authorship-scholarly-or-scientific-publications). An author has done all four of these:

1. contributed to a component of the work: its conception, design, analysis or interpretation;
2. helped write the paper, or revised it for substance;
3. approved the final version;
4. agreed to be accountable for the work.

Help that doesn't meet all four is thanked by name in the acknowledgments. Raise authorship early, and again if your role changes.

## Rules for every paper

- **Disclose AI use.** Say where you used an AI tool, and check every claim and citation it gave you.
- **Protect people and sources.** Never commit student data, raw job-ad text, private correspondence, or anyone's contact details.
- **Cut for reasons, never for results.** Record why an analysis choice was made or dropped, and whether that was before or after seeing results.
